CASE NO. 2023-CV-02156
VELOCITYSALES INC. v. JAMES HARRINGTON
United States District Court, Northern District of Illinois
Filed: March 28, 2023

OPINION AND ORDER

BACKGROUND

James Harrington ("Harrington") served as Vice President of Sales at VelocitySales Inc. ("VelocitySales") from 2017 to January 2023. During his employment, Harrington had access to proprietary customer lists, pricing structures, deal pipeline data, and sales strategies.

Before his departure, Harrington emailed himself a spreadsheet containing 5,000 enterprise customer contacts, corresponding pricing tiers, contract renewal dates, and deal pipeline information valued at approximately $12 million in potential revenue.

Harrington joined DirectCompete Corp., a direct competitor, in February 2023. Within 60 days, DirectCompete contacted 847 of VelocitySales's customers and won 23 contracts worth $3.2 million, offering pricing 10-15% below VelocitySales's rates.

VelocitySales seeks injunctive relief and damages under the Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA), 18 U.S.C. Section 1836, and the Illinois Trade Secrets Act (ITSA), 765 ILCS 1065.

ANALYSIS

I. Trade Secret Status Under DTSA

Under the DTSA, a trade secret includes "financial, business, scientific, technical, economic, or engineering information" where the owner has taken reasonable measures to keep it secret and the information derives independent economic value from not being generally known.

The court evaluates VelocitySales's customer list under the six-factor test from Learning Curve Toys: (1) extent of external knowledge; (2) extent of employee knowledge; (3) security measures; (4) value of the information; (5) effort to develop the information; (6) ease of duplication.

VelocitySales maintained the customer data in a password-protected CRM system with role-based access controls. The pricing structures were individually negotiated and not publicly available. The deal pipeline represented years of relationship-building. The court finds the customer list, pricing data, and pipeline information collectively constitute trade secrets.

II. NDA Confidentiality Coverage

Harrington signed an NDA that defined Confidential Information as "customer lists, pricing information, business strategies, financial data, and all proprietary information disclosed during employment." The NDA imposed a two-year post-employment non-disclosure obligation.

The spreadsheet Harrington emailed to himself falls squarely within the NDA's definition. Harrington's argument that the data was "in his head anyway" is without merit — the distinction is between general knowledge and specific compiled data.

III. Misappropriation

Under the DTSA, misappropriation includes acquisition by improper means and disclosure or use without consent. Harrington's act of emailing himself the spreadsheet constitutes acquisition by improper means, and DirectCompete's subsequent targeted solicitation of VelocitySales's customers using the pricing data constitutes use.

IV. Available Remedies

The DTSA provides for: (a) injunctive relief; (b) damages for actual loss and unjust enrichment; and (c) exemplary damages up to 2x actual damages for willful and malicious misappropriation, plus reasonable attorney's fees.

The court finds that Harrington's conduct was willful — he deliberately copied the data before departing for a known competitor.

ORDER

1. Preliminary injunction GRANTED: Harrington and DirectCompete are prohibited from using or disclosing VelocitySales's trade secret information.
2. Harrington shall return or destroy all copies of VelocitySales data within 7 days.
3. Damages to be determined at trial, including potential exemplary damages.
4. DirectCompete is enjoined from soliciting the 5,000 identified customers for 18 months.

SO ORDERED.
